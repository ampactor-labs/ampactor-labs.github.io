marrow / seed run 01
how people change · 14 mornings · one dinner

The room is won by what you metabolized, not what you can recite.

This morning, three minutes

  1. Open today's card below. Read it once.
  2. Say it back — out loud, like you're at a table.
  3. In LOG.md — one line taking a side with a confidence number; one line hooking it to something you've seen. Then stop. The day's idea ends.

Tap the circle when a day is done; it's remembered on this phone. Answers live in the repo's LOG.md, not here.

The cards

day 01The end of history illusion
ClaimAt every age you believe you've just now finished becoming yourself. You haven't.
ForQuoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson asked over 19,000 people to report the past decade's change and predict the next; every age group reported large past change and predicted almost none to come. The same people who laughed at their 25-year-old self were certain the 35-year-old was final.
AgainstThe reported past change might be inflated storytelling; the illusion could live in the rearview mirror, not the windshield. Day 4 makes that case.
Seen liveThe band you swore by at 25. Signing a 30-year mortgage on the taste of your current self.
DoorQuoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, "The End of History Illusion," Science, 2013. Two pages and a figure.
At the table"What were you sure of ten years ago that's just gone now?"
day 02You mature on schedule
ClaimPersonality keeps moving all life long, in a predictable direction: steadier, warmer, less anxious.
ForRoberts, Walton & Viechtbauer's meta-analysis of 92 longitudinal samples: conscientiousness and emotional stability climb through adulthood, steepest in the twenties and thirties. And it moves on demand: Hudson & Fraley measured trait change inside 16 weeks, but only in people who did trait-consistent behaviors. Goals alone did nothing. Wanting isn't doing.
AgainstRank-order stability is high. The anxious stay anxious relative to their peers; the tide lifts everyone, and your position in the fleet mostly holds.
Seen liveThe chaos friend who is now the one with the spreadsheet. On schedule, not a miracle.
DoorRoberts, Walton & Viechtbauer, Psychological Bulletin, 2006, the discussion section; Hudson & Fraley, JPSP, 2015.
At the table"Who do you know that changed the most since 25 — and did they do it, or did it happen to them?"
day 03The baseline is rubber, not stone
ClaimYou adapt back toward a set-point after most events; a few events bend the point itself.
ForThe lottery-and-accident study, read precisely: winners took less pleasure in ordinary life than controls, and paralyzed accident victims were far less unhappy than anyone predicted. Longitudinal work (Lucas) shows adaptation is real but incomplete: unemployment and widowhood leave lasting marks on the baseline.
AgainstSet-points are heritable, and heritable gets misread as immutable. Height is heritable and rose a foot with nutrition.
Seen liveThe raise that reorganized nothing by spring.
DoorLucas, "Adaptation and the Set-Point Model of Subjective Well-Being," Current Directions in Psychological Science, 2007. Four pages.
At the table"What did you buy or win that you were sure would move the needle? Did it?"
day 04You misremember who you were
ClaimMemory redraws your past self downward so the present self feels like progress.
ForWilson & Ross call it "from chump to champ": people derogate their past selves to experience improvement without changing, and the derogation grows with distance. Diaries disagree with the origin story.
AgainstMcAdams' narrative-identity work: people who tell redemption stories score higher on generativity and well-being years later. The story isn't a lie about change; sometimes it's the engine of it. Day 13 collects this debt.
Seen liveEveryone's "I used to be terrible at this" opener, delivered with visible pleasure.
DoorWilson & Ross, "From Chump to Champ," JPSP, 2001.
At the table"What's your origin story, and who'd dispute it?"
day 05Insight doesn't change you; reps do
ClaimKnowing better is a spectator sport. Behavior moves first; attitude follows it.
ForJacobson's dismantling study: behavioral activation alone, no cognitive work at all, matched full cognitive therapy for depression, and the result repeated a decade later at scale (Dimidjian). Bem's self-perception theory says why: we infer who we are from watching what we do, same as everyone else does.
AgainstReadiness is real; sometimes an insight is what unlocks the reps. And the single reorganizing insight exists; day 13 gives it its due.
Seen liveThe productivity book, reread. The mood that lifted the week the running started, not the week it was understood. Wanting isn't doing.
DoorJacobson et al., Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 1996.
At the table"What did you understand for years before you did anything about it?"
day 06Willpower is situation design
ClaimThe disciplined don't resist more temptation. They arrange to meet less of it.
ForHofmann's experience-sampling study beeped people through their days: high trait self-control predicted reporting fewer temptations, not winning more battles. The marshmallow kids who lasted covered their eyes and sang; Watts' large replication found the test's predictive power mostly dissolves once family background is controlled. The skill was never gritting; it was staging.
AgainstYou can't design away every room. Some frictions are structural, and at those margins raw persistence still earns its keep. Strategy is a skill with a ceiling.
Seen liveNo beer in the fridge beats iron will at 11pm. Deleting the app beats the screen-time dashboard.
DoorHofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs, JPSP, 2012.
At the table"What have you only ever beaten by refusing to be in the room with it?"
day 07Design for the unmotivated day
ClaimMotivation is weather. If-then plans and tiny floors are what survive it.
ForGollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis, 94 tests: implementation intentions ("when X happens, I will Y") move goal attainment with d around .65, one of the largest well-replicated levers in the literature. Fogg's model says the same from the other side: when motivation drops, only behaviors small enough to clear the floor keep happening.
AgainstFloors set low can stay low; one sentence a day is a writing career on geological time. And some changes need the spike. Nobody tiny-habits their way into a resignation letter.
Seen live"After I pour coffee, one sentence." The gym bag packed the night before, by the door, like a fire drill. Wanting isn't doing.
DoorGollwitzer & Sheeran, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 2006.
At the table"What do you do daily that no longer requires a decision?"
day 08Identity moves behavior
ClaimDurable change usually rides a noun. "I'm a runner," not "I'm trying to run."
ForBryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck framed voting as "being a voter" versus "voting" and moved real turnout by double digits in two field experiments. AA runs on the inverse of the same engine: "I'm an alcoholic" is a permanent noun deployed as maintenance technology.
AgainstA later, larger replication (Gerber and colleagues) found no effect, so hold the effect size loosely even if the mechanism is sound. And a noun without reps is a costume; day 5 said which direction the arrow runs.
Seen live"I don't smoke" versus "I'm trying to quit," said at a party. One ends the conversation; the other invites the offer.
DoorBryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck, PNAS, 2011; then Gerber et al.'s null, 2016, because the pair is the lesson.
At the table"What noun would you claim now that you couldn't have five years ago?"
day 09Change sticks at the seams
ClaimHabits die and form when context breaks: new city, new job, new decade.
ForThe fresh-start effect (Dai, Milkman & Riis): aspirational behavior spikes at temporal landmarks, Mondays to birthdays to new years. Habit research says why the deeper version works: habits run on stable cues, so relocation genuinely deletes them, and interventions delivered right after a move outperform the same interventions mid-routine (Verplanken).
AgainstSeams cut both ways; the move that kills the commute also kills the reading habit that lived inside it. And most resolutions still die by February. A seam is an opening, not a change.
Seen liveThe gym habit that finally took, the month after moving. The one that died with the old commute.
DoorDai, Milkman & Riis, "The Fresh Start Effect," Management Science, 2014.
At the table"What habit did a move kill for you? What did it create?"
day 10You become your five people
ClaimBehavior travels through networks like weather.
ForChristakis & Fowler's Framingham analyses: if a friend became obese, your own risk rose 57%; smoking cessation moved through the network in cascades, whole clusters quitting together.
AgainstShalizi & Thomas showed contagion and homophily can't be cleanly separated in that data; maybe birds of a feather just flock. The prescription survives the critique either way: choosing rooms is choosing selves, whether the room infects you or reveals you.
Seen liveThe friend group that all got into climbing inside a year. Nobody decided; everybody decided.
DoorChristakis & Fowler, New England Journal of Medicine, 2007.
At the table"Which of your habits is actually someone else's?"
day 11Witnesses hold you in place
ClaimThe people who knew the old you keep casting you in the role.
ForTeacher-expectation effects are real (Rosenthal's Pygmalion), and the honest reading after four decades of fighting (Jussim & Harber) is: modest on average, strongest on people already carrying a label. Which is exactly the family-table case; roles snap back where the casting is oldest.
AgainstWitnesses hold good identities too. The crew that knows you as the sober one is load-bearing, and commitment devices work partly because someone is watching.
Seen liveThirty-four years old, home for the holidays, seventeen by dinner.
DoorJussim & Harber, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 2005.
At the table"Around whom are you most stuck being who you used to be?"
day 12Acceptance precedes change
ClaimPeople change when they're safe, not when they're shamed.
ForRogers said it as a paradox: "when I accept myself just as I am, then I can change." The therapy-outcome literature backs the shape; the alliance predicts outcomes across wildly different schools (Wampold), and the first stable partnership tracks falling neuroticism in longitudinal work (Neyer & Asendorpf). Safety isn't the reward for change. It's the soil.
AgainstSafety can entrench; enabling has its own literature. And cold contingency works: contingency management, paying addicts for clean tests, outperforms most addiction treatments. Sometimes the soil is a consequence.
Seen liveThe person who got help after being loved through the worst year, not yelled through it.
DoorRogers, On Becoming a Person, 1961, chapter 1.
At the table"Has anyone ever actually changed because you argued them into it?"
day 13Rock bottom is mostly myth
ClaimChange is relapse-shaped. The epiphany is the exception that gets the memoir.
ForThe stages-of-change model was drawn as a spiral on purpose; relapse is inside the model, not a failure of it. One careful estimate puts the average number of quit attempts before a smoker succeeds around 30 (Chaiton); the exact number is contested, the shape isn't. And day 4 told you who writes the lightning-bolt versions.
AgainstMiller & C'de Baca documented sudden transformations, interviewed the people, and followed up years later: the changes held. Rare isn't fake.
Seen liveThe quiet quit that took nine tries, next to the conversion story everyone retells.
DoorMiller & C'de Baca, Quantum Change, 2001, first chapter. Read the counterargument's own book; that's the habit this thread is for.
At the table"Do you know anyone who changed overnight and stayed changed?"
day 14Change is a settlement
ClaimThe old self isn't erased. It's rerouted around, and it waits in its old rooms.
ForBouton's relapse work: extinction doesn't unlearn the old response, it lays new learning over it, and the old response returns in the original context. That's renewal, and it's why ten years quit can crave one specific bar.
AgainstDay 8 praised the permanent noun; this card is where it bills you. "Once an addict" protects some people and leashes others, and some recovery frameworks drop the label for exactly that reason. Both effects are real. Which one, for whom, is judgment.
Seen liveThe hometown accent coming back at the airport gate.
DoorBouton, "Context, Ambiguity, and Unlearning," Biological Psychiatry, 2002; or Wood's Good Habits, Bad Habits, the context chapter.
At the table"What part of an old you still lives in one specific place?"

Table sessions

Twice during the run, paste a persona from the repo's TABLE.md into a fresh Claude session: the skeptic in week one, the tired date in week two. Ten minutes; the scorecard needs verbatim quotes to say yes.

Day 1 says you believe you've just now finished becoming yourself. Fourteen days from now the claim is smaller and harder: you never finish becoming yourself; you resettle, room by room, in front of witnesses, on reps.
